# Mentor Calibration Guide
This guide is meant to help all industry mentors understand the parameters of their role, create consistency across mentor-mentee relationships, and support the students at a level appropriate for their individual needs. Mentors can sometimes enter the mentorship program with unrealistic expectations for the type of relationship or support the average student may need. Our goal is to ensure every student and mentor has a positive experience and fully understands their role and responsibilities in the relationship. This guide will help to align expectations on what students should learn from mentorship, how mentors can actively support that learning, and what effective and intentional mentoring looks like.

## 1\. Definition of Mentors

Mentors guides and supporters of students. We do not expect mentors to act as tutors, nor do we want mentors working on homework assignments with students. Mentors are also not responsible for making decisions for the students or fixing their problems. The overarching goal of the mentor should be to support student growth rather than providing definite answers.

### Mentor Expectations & Standards

#### Consistency

Mentors are expected to set a regular cadence for meeting with their mentee and sticking to this schedule as much as possible. Mentors should be prepared for the meeting but should allow the students to lead the discussion topics and decide what the meeting agenda will be. Mentors should assist students in determining the next steps and action items prior to the next meeting.

Review the toolkit materials on sample meeting agendas, conversation topics, and other topics to help guide your conversations as needed.

#### Respect and Inclusivity

Mentors are expected to provide a supportive and respectful environment for each student. Recognize that each student arrives in college with differing levels of prior exposure to tech, professional experience, confidence, and access to resources. The most effective mentoring relationships are those that allow the student to be honest about their prior knowledge and learn as much as they can from the mentor.

Lead conversations with kindness and understanding rather than judgement. Most students join this program because they know there are plenty of things they don't know about the working world. Instead of assuming the students know things, validate their experience and guide them towards understanding. Equity means making expectations visible and accessible to all students.

#### Boundaries

Mentors provide advice and feedback to students. Mentors do not act as tutors or as academic advisors. Avoid telling students what courses to take, how to complete their assignments, or other advice that would cross the boundary from mentor to academic advisor.

## 2\. Communication & Tone Guidelines for Mentors

Students have access to a Professional Communication Guide for those that have never had to send emails or messages in a professional setting. While we do not expect all communication between mentors and mentees to be stiff and formal, we do ask that mentors model the communication standards expected by their own workplace. Mentors should use a supportive and respectful tone with students. Provide feedback on students' progress in a clear and encouraging way, without being dismissive or overly critical.

## 4\. Indicators of Effective Mentoring

- Student engages more over time
- Student takes initiative in leading the meetings and conversations
- Student reflects more deeply on their progress and develops more insight into their own development
- Student asks more questions
- Student sets clearer, more realistic goals

## 5\. Mentor Development Mindset
Mentorship is a skill. You should expect to grow and improve over time as you learn from your experiences. You will encounter challenges along the way. Effective mentors are adaptable and reflect on the past to improve in the future.
