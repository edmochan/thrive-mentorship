# Inclusive Mentorship
Students will enter this mentorship with varying levels of prior experience, access to opportunities, confidence, and familiarity with professional norms. It is important to remember that these differences are usually not related to the student's ability or enthusiasm, but rather their life experience and exposure to the hidden curriculum. Equity in mentoring and advising hinges on ensuring that all students have access to the knowledge, support, and opportunities needed to succeed in their careers.

## What is inclusive mentoring?

Mentoring should always be a welcoming, respectful, and supportive environment for the students. However, truly inclusive mentoring should go a bit deeper to recognize the barriers to access that individual students face, and help them to address those barriers. Students come to college with differing backgrounds and experiences. Many will not have had any previous exposure to internships, networking, professional norms, and tech or workplace culture. Some will struggle with self-confidence and belonging and will not feel comfortable speaking to strangers or asking questions that might be thought of as "basic" or "easy". Many students also have jobs (sometimes even full-time employment), caretaking responsibilities, or other drains on their time and resources that detract from schoolwork or extracurriculars. Each student begins at a different starting point, and the most successful mentors recognize these disparities and allow the student to ask questions without judgement.

#### How can a mentor address this?

It is important not to make assumptions about the students' knowledge or ability too early in the relationship. Some mentors make the mistake of assuming that silence indicates lack of interest, rather than realizing silence more likely indicates lack of confidence on the student's part. Mentorship becomes most effective when mentors lead with understanding and compassion. Ask questions of the student and actively listen to their responses. Clarify expectations with the student around meeting style and cadence and what each person hopes to get out of the relationship. Be open with the student about your own experiences, successes, challenges, and failures.

## Hidden Curriculum of Tech Culture and Workplaces

One of the most important practices in providing intentional and equitable mentorship for students is making visible the "hidden curriculum" of the workplace. A hidden curriculum is the unwritten rules and professional norms that we often assume someone knows, but generally remain invisible, confusing, and largely inaccessible to students coming from less privileged backgrounds. A major goal of this mentorship program is to provide visible solutions to these invisible barriers to success.

For example, students will often hear that they should be networking more. But they may not even be able to define what networking is or recognize it when they experience it. Explain the purpose of networking, how to get involved in events that promote professional networking, how to maintain and grow a professional relationship, etc. The more explicit you can be explaining these things to the student, the more they are likely to benefit from the conversation.

Job applications and interviews are another area that tends to be rife with hidden curriculum concepts that a mentor can clarify for the student. Students will hear the importance of tailoring a resume or cover letter for each job application, but they may not understand what that truly means. They may think that if they are applying to two jobs with similar job titles, they can send identical resumes and cover letters to each. Describe to the student what specific skills and roles you would want to see on a resume for an entry-level position. Consider having the student set goals related to their job application materials, and review what they have through your lens. Give constructive feedback where you can and follow up with the student at future meetings.

Equity comes from making the invisible visible, accessible, and possible. Reflect on what you wish you had known when you started your career, or what gaps you have seen in recent hires in your workplace, and try to make these things explicit for your mentee.

## Creating an Inclusive Mentoring Environment

Inclusive environments should be encouraging, open, and honest. Encourage the student to ask questions and provide answers without judgement. Try to avoid using too much jargon in your responses. Undergraduate students are unlikely to be familiar with acronyms or the names of specific software packages but may not be comfortable admitting they do not know these things. Explain terms where you can, even before the student asks the question.

Normalize the student's uncertainty or insecurity and let them know that they belong in tech. Listen actively to what they say (and what they do not say!) Try to be patient with the students, especially early in the relationship, as they are traversing professional norms and relationships for the first time.

### Strategies for Inclusive Mentoring

#### 1\. Check for Understanding

If you are describing a situation at work or explaining a concept to a student, check in with them to see if they are following your discussion. Often we will ask "Does that make sense?" and a student can feel internal pressure to say "yes", even if they are confused. Consider changing your wording to something like "What questions do you have for me?" or "How would you explain that concept in your own words?" to check for actual understanding and encourage questions along the way.

#### 2\. Provide Structure

While some students will thrive in an informal mentorship setting, others need structure to feel comfortable. Consider beginning the relationship with a more structured format and easing up where and when it feels right. Set clear expectations for when and how you will meet, lead by example in your written and oral communication style, and provide examples for the student to help them relate your conversation topics to the workplace.

#### 3\. Validate Effort and Growth

Students are not always great at realizing the amount of progress they have made or the strengths that they have. You can help them by explicitly recognizing and validating the progress they have made throughout the year. #### 4\. Share Your Own Story

Students will benefit from hearing your own learning process and your journey to your current position. What challenges did you face along the way? What weaknesses did you have when applying for your first job, and how did you overcome those? What choices have you made along the way in your career path that you are most proud of, and what might you do differently? Describe your story and normalize your mistakes.

## Recognizing and Responding to Barriers

#### Common barriers students may face

- Financial constraints
- Time constraints
- Conflicting priorities (school, work, family, etc)
- Lack of a sense of belonging in a professional environment
- Imposter syndrome
- Limited exposure to professional environments
- Lack of access to professional networks

#### Inclusive mentoring responses

- Listen without judgment
- Acknowledge challenges
- Offer guidance and perspective
- Focus on what the student _can_ control
- Be flexible as needed
- Reach out to the student's advisor or college if you have reason to be concerned

## Inclusive Networking & Opportunities

Many students avoid networking and professional events because they feel uncomfortable and assume they do not belong in these spaces. You can help the students to understand that they **do** belong. Consider practicing elevator pitches, personal narratives, or other conversation prompts with the students to help them feel more comfortable in a networking setting. Acknowledge their discomfort and share your experience working through that. Build their confidence through practice and constructive feedback.

## Mentor Self-Reflection

Self-reflection is a critical step in growth and development. We often think about its importance for the students but forget that reflection is just as important for the mentor. After conversations with your mentee, think about the content of your discussion.

- What aspect of the hidden curriculum did you make visible during this meeting?
- What growth are you seeing from the student? Are they being more open and vocal during the discussion? Are they taking more initiative or showing more confidence in your interactions?
- What challenges did you face in this meeting? How can you address that in the next meeting?

Inclusive mentoring is a skill that can grow over time. We have a guide for reflection on the mentor-mentee relationship to help you keep track of your own progress as well.
